When to plant tomatoes after your last frost date

By Which Frost. Published 19 Nov 2026.

Tomato transplants go out after frost danger has passed, not on the NOAA median last frost date itself. Extensions describe that wait as "after frost danger," and the calendars this site has transcribed put tomatoes about 1 to 2 weeks (New Hampshire) or 2 or more weeks (Colorado) after the average last spring frost.

Frost dates on Which Frost are 1991 to 2020 NOAA medians, not a forecast for this year. A cold spring can still freeze after the 50 percent date. This is not agricultural advice.

What extensions say about tomato timing

UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions: Tomatoes (checked 19 Nov 2026) says tomatoes are a warm weather crop and tells gardeners to wait until the danger of frost has passed before putting plants in the ground.

UC ANR Publication 8159, Growing Tomatoes in the Home Garden (checked 19 Nov 2026) says to sow seed indoors 6 to 8 weeks before the last spring frost date for your area, harden plants off for a week to 10 days, and transplant after the danger of frost has passed.

Texas A&M AgriLife, Tomatoes Path to the Plate (HORT-PU-163, original publish date September 2023; checked 19 Nov 2026) says to set out spring tomato plants after the danger of frost has passed or be prepared to cover them, and for fall crops to plant about 100 days before the first expected frost.

On this site, the only tomato day counts we print are from calendars we transcribed. CSU Extension GardenNotes #720 (reviewed September 2024) lists tomato with very tender crops, typically 2 or more weeks after the average last spring frost. The UNH Extension vegetable timing fact sheet (retrieved 16 Nov 2026) says tomato transplants are safe 1 to 2 weeks after the last frost, with seed started 6 to 8 weeks before transplanting. Those counted windows appear on Colorado and New Hampshire city pages.

How to use a frost date for tomatoes

  1. Find your city on Which Frost and read the median last spring 32°F date.
  2. Check the 10 percent spring date on the same page. Planting on the 50 percent date still leaves a later freeze possible in the normals record.
  3. If your state has a transcribed calendar on this site (Colorado, Georgia month list, or New Hampshire), use that page's tomato or warm season row. Otherwise follow your state extension's tomato guide, which will usually say to wait until frost danger has passed.
  4. Watch a short range forecast the week you transplant. Extensions that mention covering plants are describing frost protection, not a different frost date.

Frequently asked questions

When should I plant tomatoes after the last frost?

University extensions treat tomatoes as a warm season crop. UF/IFAS says to wait until the danger of frost has passed. UC ANR Publication 8159 says to sow seed indoors 6 to 8 weeks before the last spring frost date and to transplant after frost danger has passed. Texas A&M AgriLife Path to the Plate says to set out spring transplants after the danger of frost has passed, or be ready to cover them. On this site, Colorado's transcribed CSU guide places tomato in a very tender group typically 2 or more weeks after the average last spring frost, and New Hampshire's transcribed UNH chart says transplants are safe 1 to 2 weeks after the last frost.

Is the NOAA 50 percent frost date safe enough for tomatoes?

No. The 50 percent date is a median for 1991 to 2020, not a promise that the last freeze is over. City pages also show the 10 percent spring date, which is later. Extensions that say wait until frost danger has passed are pointing at that caution, not at planting on the median day itself.

Where do I look up my frost date on Which Frost?

Open a city page from the home search or a state hub. The headline date is NOAA's 50 percent last spring freeze at 32°F at the nearest station with a frost product. Frost dates are medians, not guarantees.
